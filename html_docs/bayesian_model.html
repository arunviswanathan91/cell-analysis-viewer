Section 01

What is Bayesian Statistics?

Bayesian statistics is about updating a belief when new evidence arrives. It starts with some prior expectation, incorporates collected data, and revises the belief into a more informed posterior. Crucially, instead of one single answer (like a p-value), the result is a full probability distribution of possible answers — indicating not just the most likely answer, but how uncertain that estimate is.

🔥 The Clinical Diagnosis Analogy

A patient walks in with a cough. Before any test, the doctor has a prior belief: "Most coughs are just colds — probably nothing serious." The chest X-ray comes back with a suspicious shadow. The doctor updates: "Okay, now I think there's a 30% chance of something more serious." The biopsy returns benign. Update again: "Probably a harmless lesion." Each piece of evidence shifts the belief. That final shifted belief is the posterior. Bayesian modeling is this process — mathematically formalized.

Figure 1 — Bayesian Updating: Prior → Data → Posterior
The wide blue dashed curve is the prior — uncertain, before any data. The green dotted curve represents what the data alone suggests. The narrow red posterior is their combination: more precise, shifted toward the evidence. The 95% HDI bracket shows the range of most probable effect sizes after combining both.
📐 The Core Bayesian Equation
Posterior ∝ Prior × Likelihood
Posterior
The updated belief after seeing the data. A probability distribution — not a single number. This is what the model produces for every effect size.
Prior
The belief before seeing the data. In this study, encoded as sigma values (0.18–1.5) controlling how wide each level of the model is allowed to vary.
Likelihood
How well different effect sizes actually explain the data collected from 140 PDAC patients.
∝
"Is proportional to" — the right side determines the shape of the posterior. The model finds the posterior that best balances prior and data.
✗ Regular Statistics (p-value)
  • One single answer: significant or not
  • Cannot quantify magnitude of effect
  • Treats n=18 the same as n=180
  • No uncertainty quantification
  • Each test treated independently
✓ Bayesian Statistics
  • Full distribution of probable effects
  • Quantifies both size and direction
  • Priors protect against n=18 overclaiming
  • HDI gives uncertainty range for every estimate
  • Hierarchical pooling shares information
Section 02

What Makes it "Hierarchical"?

The data has a natural nested structure. Signatures live inside cell types. Cell types live inside compartments. And all of this lives inside patients. Treating every comparison as fully independent ignores this structure and wastes information. Hierarchical modeling exploits the structure through partial pooling — related things share information with each other.

🔥 Multi-Centre Drug Trial Analogy

Testing a drug across 10 hospitals. Hospital A has 5 patients, Hospital B has 50. Analyzing Hospital A alone gives unreliable results. Pooling everyone ignores real hospital differences. Hierarchical modeling does both at once: hospitals are distinct but related — Hospital A borrows statistical strength from the overall pattern. In this study, the 18 obese patients aren't analyzed in isolation — they benefit from the pattern seen across all 140 patients and all cell types in the same compartment.

Figure 2 — Four Nested Levels of the Hierarchical Model
Each level informs the one below it via priors, and individual measurements feed back upward. This "partial pooling" means the 18 obese patients are not analyzed in isolation — they benefit from patterns across all 140 patients and all related signatures in their compartment.
Section 03

The Model Formula — Decoded

Model A — Categorical (Comparing Groups)

📐 Categorical Model
μ_ij = α_j + γ_p[i] + β_OW_kj · I(BMI ∈ OW) + β_OB_kj · I(BMI ∈ OB)
Expected Score = Baseline + Patient Offset + Overweight Effect + Obese Effect
μ_ij
The predicted signature score for patient i in cell type j. The whole formula tries to predict this number for every patient × signature × cell type.
α_j
Alpha — Baseline. Expected score in normal-weight patients for cell type j. Think of this as the control group reference value. Prior: Normal(0, 1.5) — wide prior because we don't know the baseline in advance.
γ_p[i]
Gamma — Patient Offset. Each patient's unique biology unrelated to BMI. Patient 7 might just naturally express everything 0.4 Z-scores higher. Without this term, that would get incorrectly attributed to their BMI group.
β_OW_kj
Beta OW — The Main Finding. How much does signature k in cell type j change in overweight vs normal? Positive = upregulated. Negative = downregulated. Near zero = no change. This is what the paper reports.
β_OB_kj
Beta OB — The Obese Finding. Same but for obese vs normal. Together β_OW and β_OB reveal non-linear patterns — e.g., NK cells activated in OW (positive β) then collapsed in OB (near-zero β).
I(BMI ∈ OW)
Indicator Function — an on/off switch. Equals 1 if the patient is overweight, 0 otherwise. This routes each patient to the right beta. It is not a statistical test — just a filter.

Model B — Continuous (Dose-Response)

📐 Continuous Model
μ_ij = α_j + γ_p[i] + β_slope_kj · BMI_std,i
β_slope_kj
Beta Slope — The Dose-Response. For every 1 standard deviation increase in BMI (~5 units), how much does the signature change? Key finding: Tumor Classical cells showed progressive KRAS, glycolysis, and mitochondrial signatures scaling continuously with BMI — invisible to the categorical model.
BMI_std,i
Standardized BMI. Patient's BMI converted to Z-score (mean=0, SD=1 across all 140 patients). Makes slopes comparable across all signatures on the same scale regardless of original BMI units.
🔬 qPCR Analogy — Categorical vs Continuous

The categorical model is like a Western blot with three lanes — Normal, Overweight, Obese — comparing band intensities between groups. But it cannot show what happens between the groups, or whether the change is truly gradual.

The continuous model is like a dose-response curve — titrating BMI from low to high and measuring the response at each level. This is how the study detected that oncogenic KRAS signatures in Tumor Classical cells accumulate continuously with BMI — the categorical model grouped patients into bins and missed this gradual accumulation entirely.

📐 Patient Random Intercept
γ_p = γ̃_p · σ_patient, γ̃_p ~ Normal(0, 1), σ_patient ~ HalfNormal(0.50)
Why γ_p?
Each patient contributes one observation per signature across multiple cell types. Without this term, within-patient correlation inflates model confidence — making weak effects look stronger than they are. This is called pseudoreplication.
Non-centered
Writing γ_p = γ̃_p × σ_patient separates the direction (γ̃_p) from the magnitude (σ_patient) of each patient's deviation. This mathematical trick makes the MCMC sampler dramatically more efficient — like simplifying an equation before solving it.
Section 04

How the Calculation Works — MCMC and NUTS

The Bayesian formula specifies what is being calculated. MCMC is how the computer actually calculates it — because the math is too complex to solve directly for models with hundreds of parameters.

🗺 Mapping a Hilly Landscape Blindfolded

Picture someone blindfolded on a hilly landscape, tasked with mapping where the peaks and valleys are, able only to feel the ground underfoot. Strategy: wander randomly for thousands of steps. Time is naturally spent more often near peaks (the terrain holds the walker there). After enough steps, the record of "places visited" builds a map — places visited most frequently are the highest probability regions.

In this study, the landscape is the space of all possible effect sizes for all 2,143 signatures. The peaks are where the most probable effect sizes live. The map of visited locations becomes the posterior distribution.

Figure 3 — Good vs Poor Chain Convergence
Left panel (good convergence, R-hat ≈ 1.00): Four chains start from different random positions but quickly settle into the same region — all four walkers mapped the same landscape. Right panel (poor convergence, R-hat > 1.02): Chains explore different regions and never agree — results are unreliable and must be treated as exploratory only.
Standard MCMC (Basic Random Walk)

Takes random steps in random directions. Often doubles back and revisits areas already mapped. Very slow to explore the full landscape. Can get stuck in narrow probability valleys for thousands of steps.

NUTS — Used in This Study

Uses the gradient (slope) of the probability landscape to take larger, more purposeful steps. Automatically stops before making a U-turn — because retracing adds no new information. Target acceptance rate: 99%. Far more efficient.

📐 Chain Setup
4 chains × (2,000 tuning + 2,000 sampling steps) = 8,000 total samples
4 chains
Four independent walkers from four different random starting points. Like biological replicates — all four must agree before the result is trusted.
2,000 tuning
Warm-up phase — NUTS calibrates its step size to the landscape. These steps are discarded (like equilibration in molecular dynamics simulations). Not included in the posterior.
2,000 sampling
The real mapping phase — these 8,000 total steps (4 × 2,000) build the posterior distribution. Every step visited is one sample from the posterior.
Target = 0.99
NUTS accepts 99% of proposed steps. Each step is carefully chosen to be in a genuinely useful direction — almost no wasted motion.

Checking the Calculation — Convergence Diagnostics

R-hat — The Agreement Score

📐 R-hat Conceptual Formula
R-hat = Variation BETWEEN chains ÷ Variation WITHIN chains
R-hatMeaningIn This Study
= 1.000Chains perfectly identicalIdeal
≤ 1.010Excellent — Primary statusMost OB vs N comparisons ✅
1.010–1.020Marginal — interpret with cautionOW vs N non-immune: 1.012 ⚠️
> 1.020Poor — Exploratory onlyOW vs N immune coarse: 1.018–1.029 ❌

ESS — Effective Sample Size

🔬 Correlated vs Independent Samples Analogy

Consider estimating the average height in Kerala by sampling 100 people — but all from one family reunion. They're related, so heights are correlated. That's technically 100 data points, but they carry far less information than 100 truly random strangers would.

MCMC has the same problem. Step 501 is right next to step 500 — they're correlated. 8,000 total steps might only carry the independent information of 600 truly random samples. That 600 is the ESS.

E-BFMI — Did the Walker Move Freely?

📐 E-BFMI Threshold
E-BFMI > 0.3 → Sampler explored adequately ✅

Biology analogy: Checks whether the MCMC walkers had their feet stuck in mud and couldn't explore freely. E-BFMI below 0.3 means the sampler was getting trapped. This study: 0.787–0.981 across all chains — all well above threshold. Walkers moved freely throughout.

Section 06

Interpreting Results — HDI and ROPE

Figure 4 — Three Possible Scenarios for the Posterior Distribution
Scenario 1 (green, left): HDI entirely outside the ROPE — effect is both statistically real AND biologically meaningful — annotated ★★. Scenario 2 (amber, center): HDI excludes zero but overlaps the ROPE — statistically credible but may be too small to matter — annotated ★. Scenario 3 (red, right): HDI includes zero — might just be noise — not annotated.

HDI — Highest Density Interval

🔬 Why HDI is Better than a Classical Confidence Interval

A 95% HDI means: "There is a 95% probability that the true effect size falls within this range." This is what most biologists intuitively think a confidence interval means — but it's only technically valid for HDI, not for classical CIs. The HDI is the region that contains the most probable values from the posterior distribution.

ROPE — Region of Practical Equivalence

🔬 Minimum Meaningful Fold Change — qPCR Analogy

In a qPCR experiment, a researcher might say: "Only fold changes above 1.5× matter — anything below that might be real but is too small to matter biologically." ROPE does exactly this for Bayesian models. It defines a "dead zone" around zero — effects within this zone are treated as practically equivalent to no effect, even if the model is very confident they're technically non-zero.

📐 Annotation System in This Study
SymbolCriterionMeaning
★95% HDI excludes zeroStatistically credible — real, not noise. But might be small.
★★HDI excludes zero + >95% posterior outside ±0.1 ROPEModerate practical significance — real AND meaningfully large.
★★★HDI excludes zero + >95% posterior outside ±0.2 ROPEStrong practical significance — real AND substantially large.
(none)HDI includes zeroNot credible — could be noise. Not reported as a finding.
⚠️ Narrow HDI ≠ Biologically Important

A narrow HDI means high precision — not a large effect. If the model is very confident the true effect is 0.04 on a Z-score scale, that is a very small effect that may not translate to meaningful protein-level or functional changes. The fibroblast findings reported in this study are precisely estimated (narrow HDI) but individually small in magnitude. The biological argument rests on their being widespread and coordinated across many signatures simultaneously — not on individual large magnitudes.

Section 07

The Prior Table — Every Number Explained

Before seeing any data, the model must know how much variation to expect at each hierarchical level. These are priors. The sigma values are "leash lengths" — how wide or narrow the prior allows things to vary.

🐕 The Leash Analogy

Short leash (small sigma): "I don't expect huge effects here. Pull the estimate back toward zero if the data suggests something extreme." This protects against overclaiming with n=18 obese patients.

Long leash (large sigma): "A wide range of values is plausible. Let the data decide." Used where there genuinely is no prior expectation — like what the baseline expression of any given signature will be.

Section 08

Stabl Then Bayesian — Not Redundant, Complementary

❓ Common Question — Isn't Both Selecting on BMI?

No. They ask fundamentally different questions. Stabl asks: which signatures are consistently related to BMI at all? Bayesian modeling asks: how much do they change, with what certainty, and is it biologically meaningful? Running both is not redundant — it is essential. Without Stabl, Bayesian modeling on all 2,143 signatures with 140 patients is computationally impossible. Without Bayesian modeling after Stabl, the result is just a list with no effect sizes or uncertainty.

Figure 5 — The Complete Two-Stage Analytical Pipeline
Stabl acts as a rapid screening test — returning only yes/no for each signature. Bayesian modeling is the detailed workup — returning full effect size distributions with uncertainty. Running both is not redundant: Stabl is what makes Bayesian modeling computationally feasible, and Bayesian modeling is what actually quantifies what Stabl found.

Section 09

Is the Cohort Big Enough? A Simulation Check

Every claim so far assumes the real cohort (N = 51 normal, 58 overweight, 18 obese) is large enough for the hierarchical model to detect a genuine BMI effect without also inventing effects that are not there. A simulation study tests this directly, using synthetic data with a known ground truth instead of the real (unknown) biology.

🔬 The Instrument Calibration Analogy

Before trusting a new assay on real patient samples, a lab runs it on a calibration standard — a sample with a known, pre-defined concentration — to confirm the assay can actually detect that concentration reliably, and does not report a signal when the true concentration is zero. The cohort-adequacy simulation plays the same role for the Bayesian model: known effects are injected into synthetic data at the real sample sizes, and the model's ability to recover them (and to stay silent when there is nothing to find) is measured directly.

📐 Simulation Setup
Structure
Same three-level hierarchy, priors, and NUTS sampler settings as the manuscript model, matched to the immune-coarse compartment: 9 cell types × 6 signatures/cell type = 54 features, with a non-centered patient-level random intercept.
Cohort sizes
Real sizes preserved exactly: N = 51 normal, 58 overweight, 18 obese — the same imbalance that makes the obese group the most fragile in the actual analysis.
Power arm
A true overweight-vs-normal effect of 0.5 SD is injected into 50% of the 54 features. The question: how often does the model's 95% HDI correctly exclude zero with the right sign?
Null arm
No effect is injected — signatures are flat across BMI by construction. The question: how often does the model's 95% HDI wrongly exclude zero (a false positive)?
Replicates
Both arms are run on 4 independently simulated datasets, each fit with the full 4-chain × 2,000-tuning + 2,000-draw NUTS sampler used throughout the study.
MetricResult
Convergence rate (both arms)100%
Detection rate, power arm (HDI excludes zero)59.3% ± 3.7%
Detection rate, power arm (correct sign)59.3% ± 3.7%
False-positive rate, null arm0.0% ± 0.0% (0/216 tests)
✓ Interpretation

At the real cohort sizes, the model has roughly 59% power to recover a true overweight-vs-normal effect of 0.5 SD — a moderate but real detection rate, not a coin flip. More importantly, the false-positive rate across 216 null tests is 0% — well below the nominal 5% — meaning the hierarchical priors are regularizing appropriately rather than manufacturing spurious effects out of noise. This is a check of statistical adequacy at this sample size, not a replication of the biological findings themselves.

⚠️ What This Does Not Show

A ~59% power means some true effects of this magnitude in the real data may go undetected (a false negative), particularly in the 18-patient obese group. It does not mean the reported findings are wrong — it means the study is conservative: the model is more likely to under-report real effects than to over-report false ones, which is the appropriate failure mode for a discovery-stage biological study.

Section 10

Quick Reference Summary

All Key Terms at a Glance

Prior
The belief about effect size BEFORE seeing data. Encoded as sigma values. Controls the "leash length" at each hierarchical level.
Posterior
Updated belief AFTER combining prior with data. A probability distribution — not a single number. This is what the model produces.
Hierarchical
Model accounts for nested structure: signatures → cell types → patients. Related measurements share information via partial pooling.
α (alpha)
Baseline signature score in normal-weight patients. The control group reference point. Prior: Normal(0, 1.5).
β (beta)
THE MAIN RESULT. How much each signature changes with BMI. Positive = up, Negative = down. Reported per cell type per comparison.
γ (gamma)
Patient-specific offset. Captures each individual's unique biology unrelated to BMI. Prevents pseudoreplication.
σ (sigma)
Controls variation allowed at each level. Smaller = tighter leash = more regularization against overclaiming.
MCMC
Algorithm that estimates the posterior by random sampling — mapping a probability landscape by wandering through it for thousands of steps.
NUTS
The specific MCMC algorithm used. Uses gradients to take smart steps, stops before U-turns. Target acceptance = 99%.
Chains (×4)
Four independent MCMC runs from different start points. Like biological replicates — all must agree before the result is trusted.
R-hat
Agreement between chains. Must be ≤ 1.01 for Primary status. > 1.02 = Exploratory only.
ESS
Effective independent samples. Must be > 400. Low ESS = unreliable estimates even if R-hat looks okay.
E-BFMI
Checks if sampler got stuck. Must be > 0.3. This study: 0.787–0.981 — all good.
HDI
95% probability range for the true effect size. If HDI excludes zero → effect is credible → annotated ★.
ROPE
Minimum biologically meaningful effect threshold. If entire HDI lies outside ROPE → practically significant → ★★ or ★★★.
Stabl
Pre-screening tool. 500 bootstrap iterations to find signatures consistently associated with BMI. Reduces 2,143 → manageable set.
Cohort simulation
In-silico adequacy check at the real N = 51/58/18 sample sizes: ~59% power to detect a 0.5 SD effect, 0% false-positive rate across 216 null tests.
🎯 The Whole Story in One Paragraph

The dataset comprises 140 PDAC patients with BMI data and 2,143 immune and stromal gene signatures across 65 cell types. Stabl screens these like a rapid skin test — 500 bootstrap iterations to find signatures robustly linked to BMI, reducing the problem to a manageable size. Bayesian hierarchical modeling then performs the detailed workup — estimating with full uncertainty how much each signature changes with BMI at both the cell type and signature level. The hierarchical structure pools information across related signatures, cell types, and patients, allowing confident statements even about the small n=18 obese group. NUTS-MCMC with 4 independent chains maps the full probability distribution of every effect size. R-hat, ESS, and E-BFMI verify each calculation was reliable — comparisons that fail these checks are clearly flagged as Exploratory. Finally, HDI identifies real effects and ROPE identifies meaningful ones. The non-linear patterns revealed — NK cells activated in overweight then collapsed in obesity, Tumor Classical cells showing dose-dependent KRAS accumulation — are findings that no standard analysis method could have detected.
